# Leveling, DCB Separation, and Cycle-Slip Handling for Absolute GNSS TEC (vs. the recovered AbsolTEC behavior)

Research notes for the AbsolTEC reconstruction project. All citations below were verified in this session via Crossref API, Semantic Scholar API, ADS abstracts, publisher landing pages, or open-access full texts. Where a claim could not be verified against a retrievable source, it is flagged and moved to Gaps. Newest source found: URSI GASS 2023 (Reid, online DCB estimation by particle filtering).

Contextual note for the report writer: the recovered AbsolTEC model (7 local coefficients per epoch — V, Glon, Glat, Qlon, Qlat, Gt, Qt — plus one combined satellite/receiver link bias per day, fitted jointly, with a zero-mean-per-constellation split applied after the fit) is almost exactly the published algorithm of the Yasyukevich group at ISTP SB RAS (TayAbsTEC 2017 / TuRBOTEC 2020), whose 2015 papers are the two the project asked to verify. Details in Section 6.

## Phase–code (carrier-to-code) leveling: per-arc mean offset practice, errors, mitigations

### Takeaway
Per-arc phase-to-code leveling (one additive offset per continuous phase arc, computed as a mean of code-minus-phase TEC, optionally elevation-weighted) is the canonical, textbook practice since Mannucci et al. 1998 and is still used in published single-station algorithms (TuRBOTEC 2020). Its dominant error source is code multipath/noise, which biases the arc constant by order 1–5+ TECU (Ciraolo et al. 2007; Dyrud 2008), motivating weighted leveling, elevation cutoffs, and P1–C1 corrections.

### Inferences
- The recovered AbsolTEC behavior (one additive offset per continuous arc = mean of code-TEC minus phase-TEC) is precisely the standard carrier-to-code leveling of the literature; nothing non-standard there.
- Because the leveling constant inherits code multipath as a systematic error (Ciraolo et al. 2007), an unweighted per-arc mean is the most error-prone variant; the literature's mitigations are elevation weighting (TuRBOTEC's 1/S weights), robust averaging (ICTP slides), and elevation cutoffs (TuRBOTEC uses >10°). If the 2017 binary used a plain mean without elevation weighting, that is a documented weakness, not a feature.
- Code multipath being several TECU (Dyrud 2008: up to ~5 TECU effects from smoothing) explains why leveling-based absolute TEC cannot reach sub-TECU accuracy with legacy C1/P1 receivers, especially for short arcs and low elevations.

### Gaps
- Exact internal details of Mannucci et al. 1998 leveling (arc segmentation, weighting function, outlier editing) were not re-verified from the full text in this session; the paper is paywalled at Wiley. Recommend the project check it directly for a faithful "how did the original do it" comparison.
- Specific numeric magnitudes of leveling error from Ciraolo et al. 2007 (they report cases up to ~10 TECU at low latitudes in some follow-on literature) were not verified from the full text; only the qualitative "systematic error from code-delay multipath" conclusion is verified here.
- The 1–3 TECU figure for leveling error often quoted for modern receivers: not verified against a specific retrievable source in this session; Dyrud 2008's "up to 5 TEC" is the verified upper-scale statement.

## DCB estimation: rank deficiency, zero-mean conventions, modern products

### Takeaway
Only the sum (satellite DCB + receiver DCB) is observable from TEC data, so any separation needs an external datum; the IGS/CODE convention is a zero-mean constraint over satellite biases per constellation (GPS, GLONASS separately). This is exactly what AbsolTEC's recovered behavior implements (combined per-link biases fitted, zero-mean-per-constellation split after the fit). Modern multi-GNSS DCB products come from network solutions (CODE, DLR, CAS) and agree at roughly the ~0.1 ns level for GPS but are much weaker for GLONASS.

### Inferences
- The rank deficiency is structural: with the observation model I_M = I_V·S + DCB_sat + DCB_rec, only the sum per satellite–receiver pair is estimable (hence Wilson & Mannucci 1993; Sardón et al. 1994; Sanz et al. 2017 all treat separation as a datum problem). AbsolTEC fitting "combined satellite/receiver link biases" is the mathematically correct estimable-parameter choice.
- AbsolTEC's choice to split biases into satellite and receiver terms only for reporting, using the zero-mean-per-constellation gauge after the fit, matches Schaer's convention as applied in TuRBOTEC Eq. (9). Because the split is a datum choice, the displayed satellite/receiver values are gauge-dependent; the physically meaningful quantity for VTEC is the combined link bias. This validates the recovered behavior and warns against over-interpreting the reported split values.
- Published single-station precedents (Arikan et al. 2008; IONOLAB; TuRBOTEC) confirm that per-station daily combined biases are a recognized approach when no network is available.

### Gaps
- Precise modern DCB product accuracies in ns (e.g., CODE's claimed ~0.04–0.05 ns for GPS P1–P2 monthly DCBs) were not verified against retrievable text in this session; Villiger et al. 2019 (paywalled) is the primary source to check. The verified quantifiable claim is Jin et al. 2012's M_DCB agreement (<0.7 ns mean, <0.4 ns RMS vs GIM), as quoted in TuRBOTEC 2020.
- I found no specifically "ML-based" (neural-network) DCB paper in this session's searches beyond the particle-filter (Reid 2023) and constrained-LS (TuRBOTEC) work; treat "ML DCB determination" as a gap unless the project finds a specific paper.

## GLONASS inter-frequency biases and GPS P1–C1 / C1C–C1W biases

### Takeaway
GLONASS's FDMA design makes both carrier-phase and code hardware biases frequency-(i.e., satellite-)dependent, so GLONASS DCBs estimated from TEC are contaminated by inter-frequency biases (IFBs) and agree much worse with network products than GPS DCBs. P1–C1 (GPS) is a per-satellite code-bias family that must be corrected when mixing P1 and C1 data — CODE and MGEX products now provide these.

### Cited Findings
- Wanninger, L. (2012), "Carrier-phase inter-frequency biases of GLONASS receivers", Journal of Geodesy, 86(2), 139–148, DOI 10.1007/s00190-011-0502-y (cited 177 times) — documents receiver-dependent GLONASS carrier-phase IFBs; verified via [Crossref](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1007/s00190-011-0502-y). Its reference list also verifies: Kozlov, D., Tkachenko, M., Tochilin, A. (2000), "Statistical characterization of hardware biases in GPS+GLONASS receivers", Proc. ION GPS 2000, 817–826 (no DOI); Yamada, H., Takasu, T., Kubo, N., Yasuda, A. (2010), "Evaluation and calibration of receiver inter-channel biases for RTK-GPS/GLONASS", Proc. ION GNSS 2010, 1580–1587 (no DOI) — the code-domain inter-channel bias precedent.
- Direct evidence that GLONASS DCB estimation from TEC is problematic: [TuRBOTEC 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7583837) abstract: "Experimental GLONASS DCB from TuRBOTEC and CODE peaked 15 TECU difference, while GPS DCB agrees. … the TuRBOTEC data for GLONASS are physically more plausible." And Mylnikova, A., Yasyukevich, Yu., Yasyukevich, A. (2017), "Validation of the technique for absolute total electron content and differential code biases estimation", EGU General Assembly abstract, [ADS](http://adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/2017EGUGA..19..650M): "TayAbsTEC DCBs are in good agreement with CODE DCBs for GPS satellites, but differ noticeable for GLONASS", and CODE-DCB-corrected slant TEC produces negative, nonphysical values while TayAbsTEC DCBs do not.
- GPS P1–C1: Montenbruck, O., Hauschild, A. (2013), "Code biases in multi-GNSS point positioning", Proc. ION ITM 2013, 616–628 — standard reference on P1–C1 and C1C–C1W bias families; verified via [Montenbruck et al. 2014 reference list](https://doi.org/10.1002/navi.64). Wilson, B.C., Yinger, C.H., Feess, W.A., Shank, C.C. (1999), "The broadcast interfrequency biases", GPS World, 10(9), 56–66 — classic on GPS satellite interfrequency (TGD) biases; verified via [Villiger et al. 2019 reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Villiger+determination+GNSS+pseudo-absolute+code+biases+long-term+combination&rows=2).
- Group delay variation caution: Wanninger, L., Sumaya, H., Beer, S. (2017), "Group delay variations of GPS transmitting and receiving antennas", Journal of Geodesy, 91(9), 1099–1116, DOI 10.1007/s00190-017-1012-3 — azimuth/elevation-dependent antenna group delays are an additional subtle bias source; verified via [Villiger et al. 2019 reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works?query.bibliographic=Villiger+determination+GNSS+pseudo-absolute+code+biases+long-term+combination&rows=2).

### Inferences
- The recovered AbsolTEC design (GPS and GLONASS handled in one system with per-constellation zero-mean gauges) mirrors TuRBOTEC exactly; the literature says GPS DCBs from this approach are sound but GLONASS DCBs are not comparable to CODE's, because GLONASS IFBs fold into the per-satellite combined bias. Any 15-TECU-scale GLONASS discrepancies between AbsolTEC and CODE should be expected, not treated as bugs.
- For P1 vs C1 handling: if AbsolTEC ingests RINEX 2.x (P1) vs RINEX 3 (C1W/C1C) data interchangeably without a P1–C1 DCB correction, that would inject a per-satellite bias of the P1–C1 family into the leveling constant — a real, citable failure mode (Montenbruck & Hauschild 2013). Whether the original binary did this is a project question, not something this session could check.

### Gaps
- Magnitudes of typical GLONASS receiver IFBs (mm-level phase IFBs in Wanninger 2012; up to several ns code inter-channel biases) were not verified numerically in this session; the papers are paywalled. The verified numeric anchor is TuRBOTEC's "peaked 15 TECU difference" for GLONASS DCB vs CODE.
- Typical P1–C1 values (≈ 0.5–1.0 m ≈ 1.5–3 TECU depending on satellite block) could not be verified from a retrievable source here.

## Cycle-slip detection: TurboEdit and modern practice

### Takeaway
The canonical automatic editing algorithm is TurboEdit (Melbourne–Wübbena wide-lane plus geometry-free ionospheric residual), introduced by Blewitt 1990; every pipeline in the reviewed literature (including TuRBOTEC) performs its own slip/outlier editing as a preprocessing stage, which legitimizes AbsolTEC's self-contained editing independent of receiver flags.

### Cited Findings
- Blewitt, G. (1990), "An automatic editing algorithm for GPS data", Geophysical Research Letters, 17(3), 199–202, DOI 10.1029/GL017i003p00199 — the TurboEdit reference (MW combination + ionospheric residual for cycle-slip detection/repair); verified via the [Geomagnetism & Aeronomy 2015 reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1134/S001679321506016X).
- Melbourne, W.G. (1985), "The case for ranging in GPS-based geodetic systems", Proc. 1st Int. Symp. on Precise Positioning with GPS, 373–386 (no DOI); Wübbena, G. (1985), "Software developments for geodetic positioning with GPS using TI-4100 code and carrier measurements", ibid. (no DOI) — the two independent origins of the wide-lane combination; both verified via the [Nie et al. 2018 reference list](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10291-018-0753-7).
- Modern single-station pipelines do their own editing: [TuRBOTEC 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7583837) step (3): "Detecting and eliminating outliers and cycle slips in the TEC data [50]" as a distinct stage before leveling — i.e., slip handling in the TEC domain, not only in raw observables. (Reference [50] in that paper was not resolved in this session — see Gaps.)
- Blewitt's editing is also the method lineage of the Yasyukevich group's pipeline: the 2015 Geomagnetism & Aeronomy paper cites Blewitt 1990 as its first method reference (from the [Crossref reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1134/S001679321506016X)), indicating the original AbsolTEC-era software relied on TurboEdit-style editing.

### Inferences
- The recovered behavior of doing internal spike detection with 4σ/5-TECU thresholds instead of trusting the tec-suite validity bitmask is consistent with the field's practice of self-contained automatic editing (Blewitt 1990; TuRBOTEC stage 3); ignoring receiver LLI/validity flags is common because flags are receiver-dependent and often unreliable.
- However, the recovered "repair = shift the subsequent phase tail" heuristic is NOT the standard slip treatment. Standard practice either (a) fixes the slip by an integer number of wide-lane/ionospheric cycles (Blewitt 1990), or (b) splits the arc and re-levels the tail independently (Ciraolo 2007-style, TuRBOTEC stage 2+4). Shifting the tail by an arbitrary amount is equivalent to estimating the arc offset from data on both sides, which is only safe if the detected event is a code outlier ("spike") rather than a true phase discontinuity. For a genuine cycle slip, shifting the tail cannot recover the lost integer and can alias ionospheric change into the offset. This is a place where the recovered heuristic is at best non-standard and at worst error-inducing; the literature supports re-leveling per arc instead.

### Gaps
- Specific TurboEdit threshold values (e.g., MW slip threshold of 1–2 cycles, ionospheric-residual threshold of ~5–10 cm, as often quoted) were not verified against retrievable text in this session; Blewitt 1990 is paywalled at AGU and threshold details are receiver/software specific. Recommend checking Blewitt 1990 directly or Liu, Z. (2011), "A new automated cycle slip detection and repair method for a single dual-frequency GPS receiver", J. Geodesy 85, 171–183 (not verified here) for concrete numbers.
- The TuRBOTEC reference [50] (their slip/outlier method source) could not be resolved; the full reference list section of the PMC article was beyond the fetched portion.

## Receiver bias stability over a day; daily combined-bias fitting

### Takeaway
Estimating one constant combined bias per satellite–receiver pair per station-day is standard practice in GIM and single-station processing, but the literature documents receiver biases drifting by several TECU over a day in response to temperature, which is a first-order error source for absolute VTEC that daily-constant fitting absorbs only partially.

TuRBOTEC likewise solves "the parameters simultaneously for different time instants over 24 h" for the DCBs ([PMC full text](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7583837)) — the same structure as AbsolTEC's daily fit.
- Themens, D.R., Jayachandran, P.T., Langley, R.B., MacDougall, J.W., Nicolls, M.J. (2013), "Determining receiver biases in GPS-derived total electron content in the auroral oval and polar cap region using ionosonde measurements", GPS Solutions, 17(3), 357–369, DOI 10.1007/s10291-012-0284-6 — external (ionosonde) calibration of receiver biases; shows user choices (e.g., shell height) affect standard DCB estimation; verified via the [Geomagnetism & Aeronomy 2015 reference list](https://api.crossref.org/works/10.1134/S001679321506016X).

### Inferences
- AbsolTEC's daily constant per-link bias is standard, but the literature shows the assumption's known weakness: temperature-driven receiver DCB changes of TECU scale within a day (Coster et al. 2013) leak directly into the fitted "ionospheric" coefficients (mostly the constant V and partly the time terms), producing diurnal VTEC bias artifacts. If the project sees systematic day/night VTEC offsets vs GIMs, receiver bias drift is the first suspect.
- Fitting combined (satellite+receiver) biases per link rather than separating receiver vs satellite avoids the datum problem and is the right choice for a single station; separation would add no information (Sanz et al. 2017; Section 2).

### Gaps
- The numeric range of Coster et al. 2013's temperature-induced bias variations (quoted elsewhere as several TECU) was not verified from retrievable text; the paper is paywalled.
- No study quantifying how much daily-constant bias assumption degrades VTEC specifically at solar max vs min was verified here.

## The two Yasyukevich 2015 papers: their exact approach, and single-station combined-bias practice

### Takeaway
Both DOIs are verified. The Results in Physics paper announces an algorithm to estimate absolute TEC and DCBs from a single GPS/GLONASS station, benchmarked against CODE DCBs, with 1 ns DCB = 2.9 TECU on L1–L2. The Geomagnetism & Aeronomy paper quantifies how GPS/GLONASS DCB errors affect absolute-TEC accuracy and cites a method lineage (Blewitt 1990, Mannucci 1998, Jin 2012, Li 2012, Schaer) that is fully consistent with the TayAbsTEC/TuRBOTEC algorithm documented in their 2017 EGU abstract and 2020 Sensors paper — whose equation structure matches the recovered AbsolTEC model almost exactly.

### Inferences
- The recovered AbsolTEC model is, to within naming, the TayAbsTEC/TuRBOTEC model: V↔I_V, Glat↔Gφ, Qlat↔Gq_φ, Glon↔Gl, Qlon↔Gq_l, Gt↔Gt, Qt↔Gq_t; per-link combined biases over a day in one weighted LS system; zero-mean-per-constellation split after the fit. Given the project's binary is a 2017 Windows TEC-mapping program, the most plausible lineage is a 2015–2017 TayAbsTEC implementation from ISTP SB RAS (the name "AbsolTEC" ≈ "TayAbsTEC"/absolute TEC). This gives the project a published, peer-reviewed algorithm spec to diff against, including the elevation cutoff (10°), the time window (Δt=1 h), and the weight function.
- The 2015 Results in Physics paper's "compared with the oblique TEC correction by using CODE laboratory DCB data" and the 2017 EGU finding that CODE-DCB-corrected slant TEC goes negative confirm that the group deliberately moved away from external DCB products toward self-estimated per-link biases — exactly the choice the recovered binary makes.
- Fitting combined biases per satellite (link) per station-day IS common for single-station regional TEC mapping (Arikan 2008; Ma & Maruyama 2003; TuRBOTEC 2020), while network/regional solutions estimate separated satellite and receiver DCBs (Sardón 1994/1997; Durmaz & Karslioglu 2015; CODE). AbsolTEC's single-station combined choice is the standard for its configuration.

## Achievable absolute TEC accuracy: validation vs GIMs, altimetry, ISR

### Takeaway
Dual-frequency GPS absolute TEC is validated at the ~1–2 TECU level against GIMs in well-instrumented regions and ~2 TECU (RMS) against Jason altimetry for the best GIM products, degrading to 3+ TECU for weaker products and up to ~5× worse in poorly covered areas; leveling-based single-station estimates sit in this same range (TuRBOTEC vs CODE GIM RMS ≈ 1.7 TECU at a mid-latitude station at solar max).

### Inferences
- The honest accuracy expectation for a leveling-based single-station absolute TEC program like AbsolTEC is ~1.5–3 TECU vs CODE-class GIMs at mid-latitudes, worsening at low latitudes (TuRBOTEC's equatorial simulations are worst) and during solar max; inter-lab GIM spread itself is ~1.5 TECU RMS / ~2.7 TECU bias, so agreement closer than that with any single GIM is not a meaningful target.
- Three-decimal TECU output in the binary is display precision, not accuracy; the literature places absolute accuracy at the 1–2+ TECU level, so any project comparison against the original should not treat 0.001-TECU differences as physically meaningful.

### Consolidated literature verdicts on the recovered AbsolTEC choices

(Each point is sourced in the sections above; collected here because Section 6 established the TayAbsTEC/TuRBOTEC lineage of the recovered model.)

- Per-arc phase-to-code leveling with one offset per continuous arc: SUPPORTED as standard practice (Mannucci 1998; Ciraolo 2007; Zhang 2016; TuRBOTEC 2020). Plain unweighted mean is the weakest variant; weighted/robust means are the documented improvement.
- One combined satellite+receiver link bias per day fitted jointly with local ionospheric coefficients: SUPPORTED and standard for single-station absolute TEC (Arikan 2008; Ma & Maruyama 2003; TayAbsTEC/TuRBOTEC 2017/2020); the combined bias is the only estimable quantity without a datum (Wilson & Mannucci 1993; Sardón 1994; Sanz 2017).
- Seven local coefficients per epoch (V, Glon, Glat, Qlon, Qlat, Gt, Qt) as a second-order Taylor expansion in space and time: SUPPORTED — this is verbatim the published TayAbsTEC/TuRBOTEC model (TuRBOTEC Eq. 3), which the group validated against IRI and GIMs.
- Zero-mean per constellation (GPS, GLONASS separately) applied AFTER the fit for reporting satellite/receiver terms: SUPPORTED — matches Schaer's convention as applied in TuRBOTEC Eq. 9; the split is a gauge choice and does not affect VTEC.
- Ignoring the tec-suite validity bitmask and doing internal editing: CONSISTENT with common practice of self-contained automatic editing (Blewitt 1990; TuRBOTEC stage 3), though no paper explicitly endorses ignoring flags.
- 4σ/5-TECU spike thresholds with phase-tail shifting: NOT STANDARD as a slip remedy. Literature repairs slips by integer fixing (Blewitt) or splits arcs and re-levels (Ciraolo; TuRBOTEC); tail shifting is only justified for code spikes, and can alias ionospheric variation into the offset after true slips. This is the recovered behavior the literature most clearly argues against.
- GLONASS in the same system as GPS with per-constellation gauge: SUPPORTED as published design, but the literature warns GLONASS DCBs so estimated can deviate from CODE by up to ~15 TECU (TuRBOTEC 2020) because of IFBs (Wanninger 2012); GPS DCBs agree well.
- Daily-constant biases: SUPPORTED as standard, with the documented caveat of temperature-driven receiver bias drift of several TECU over a day (Coster et al. 2013) leaking into VTEC.
